The Atom: A History

Nobody has ever seen an atom directly — yet we know what's inside one. This lesson follows 2,400 years of thinkers and experiments that built, broke, and rebuilt our model of the atom.

Learning intentions

By the end of this lesson you can: (1) name the key scientists who developed atomic theory, (2) describe how each experiment changed the model of the atom, and (3) explain why scientific models change when new evidence appears.

1. What is everything around you — this screen, the air, you — made of?

Matter — and all matter is made of atoms. A single drop of water contains about 1.5 sextillion of them.

2. Name one particle that is smaller than an atom.

Protons, neutrons and electrons are the three subatomic particles you need this year.

3. If nobody has ever seen an atom with their eyes, how could scientists possibly know what one looks like?

Indirect evidence. Scientists fire things at atoms, pass electricity through gases, and measure what happens — then build a model that explains the results. That's exactly what this lesson is about.

The big idea

A scientific model is not a photograph — it's our current best explanation of the evidence. When new evidence turns up that the model can't explain, the model has to change. The atom's model has changed at least five times.
